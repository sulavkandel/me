## Q1. Why do you want to be part of YIC 2026? (<=250 words)
I am applying because I want to grow from someone who builds technology into someone who uses it to solve real problems for people. I come from Nepal, where I have seen how uneven access to opportunity holds back talented young people. My engineering work, from optimising live networks at Nepal Telecom to building a blockchain voting system that protects one person and one vote, has always been about trust and fairness, not only code.

YIC 2026 draws me because it puts dialogue and diplomacy at the centre. Over the past year I have taught AI tools to learners from very different backgrounds, and I keep seeing that the hardest part of change is not the technology. ## Q2. Biography (<=250 words)
My name is Sulav Kandel, and I am a final year Electronics, Communication and Information Engineering student at Tribhuvan University in Nepal. I grew up in Kathmandu and have always been the kind of person who takes things apart to see how they work, which is probably how I ended up in engineering.

Most of what I do sits where technology meets people. I recently interned at Nepal Telecom, working on live mobile network testing and optimisation. Alongside my studies I teach AI content creation at a skill development centre, helping people from all sorts of jobs use new tools without feeling left behind. I have also built projects I genuinely care about, including SolVote, a blockchain voting system designed to make elections fair and tamper proof, and a machine learning model that helps mobile networks balance traffic more efficiently.

Away from tech, I volunteer with Nurture Nari and Subharambha Nepal, two community groups focused on women's health and empowerment. That work matters to me because impact is not only about big ideas, it is about showing up for your own community.

I am curious, a little stubborn about finishing what I start, and happiest when I am learning something new with other people. My long term goal is to build technology in Nepal that solves local problems and can still hold its own on a global stage. The Youth Impact Conference feels like a natural next step toward that.
